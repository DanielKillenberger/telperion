### Formal description

> Ash. Oleaceae. _Fraxinus excelsior_ L. ( _F. coriariifolia_ Scheeleis) is a deciduous tree with a narrow crown, 12–18 m tall and exceptionally reaching 43 m, often reduced to a shrub in exposed positions. Bark grey, smooth in young trees, furrowed in mature trees. Buds black, terminal 5–10 mm long. Leaves opposite, although one leaf of a pair may be displaced vertically by up to 16 mm or, more rarely, can be missing (Boodle [1915](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0066)), to 30 cm long, pinnate, typically < 5000 mm2 (Grime, Hodgson & Hunt [2007](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0230)) with up to thirteen sessile, opposite leaflets, glabrous or nearly so, exstipulate. Leaflets usually _c_. 7 cm, lanceolate to ovate, apiculate to acuminate, serrate. Trioecious or subdioecious with male, hermaphrodite and female trees (Wallander [2001](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0626); Tal [2006](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0581)). Flowers in short axillary panicles of > 100 flowers, opening before the leaves, purplish, devoid of perianth. Hermaphrodite flowers consist of one ovary and two inferior purple stamens: protogynous and self-fertile (Morand-Prieur _et al_. [2003](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0428); FRAXIGEN [2005](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0199)). In male flowers, ovary abortive; in female flowers, anthers abortive or rudimentary. Syncarpous ovary contains four ovules but usually only one (rarely two) is fertilized. Fruit a samara about 3–4 cm long, pale brown when ripe.

### 5.1 Gregariousness

> Occasionally as scattered trees, but often in clumps, and sometimes ash may form nearly pure stands (Wardle [1961](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0630)).

### 5.2 Performance in various habitats

> _Fraxinus excelsior_ grows fastest and attains its largest dimensions on sites that are fertile and moist but well drained with the winter water-table 40–100 cm below the surface (Kerr & Cahalan [2004](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0333)). In mainland Europe, this can be of sufficiently high density and vitality that ash can be considered invasive leading to ‘fraxinization’ where ash takes over from other competitive species (see FRAXIGEN [2005](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0199) and Dobrowolska _et al_. [2011](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0147) for references). Exposure to strong winds and soil dryness or compaction reduces the rate of height and diameter growth and the ultimate size of the tree (Wardle [1961](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0630)), but ash is comparatively wind firm compared to competitor trees (Rackham [2014](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0508)). Under extreme conditions, it remains a shrub. Good stands of ash occur over the pH range of 5–8 (Wardle [1961](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0630)) and it performs poorly on acidic sites. When grown on acidic sand, seedling establishment is low, and in southern Sweden under a canopy of _Picea abies_ partial felling of the spruce did not improve height or diameter growth of newly planted ash seedlings unlike in _Fagus sylvatica_, _Quercus robur_ and _Tilia cordata_. Mortality was also significantly higher in ash. Ash will sometimes establish on sites with compacted clay or very dry soils over chalk, but the seedlings generally do poorly and do not survive beyond 3–4 years (Tabari, Lust & Neirynk [1998](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0580)).

### 6.1 Morphology, branching, maturity and size

> Branching of the shoot is monopodial with a low number of branch orders (3–5). Ash invests in vertical height growth with rapid self-pruning of side branches while young. As it approaches maturity at 50–90 years (Claessens _et al_. [1999](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0110)), ash is typically 7–25 m tall and the canopy increases in width to eventually form a rounded rhombic crown up to 8 m diameter. In the south of England, the crown of ash is consistently wider than those of any of its main competitor trees for all trunk diameters (Hemery, Savill & Pryor [2005](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0267)) as befits a light-demanding adult tree. Hein & Spiecker ([2008](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0260)) give allometric equations for growth of open-grown trees. On rich alluvial soils, ash can reach 40–45 m in height and 1.6–1.8 m dbh, but in lowland forests, 20–25 m height is common (Huldén [1941](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0285); Collin & Badot [1997](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0118)). Female trees generally are shorter and have thinner trunks than males (Tal [2006](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0581)); Rohmeder ([1967](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0521)) recorded that male trees in Germany were 14% wider, 10% taller and 40% larger in volume than female trees. Male trees may be bigger due to a lower investment in reproduction although Tal ([2006](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0581)) argues that male costs may be as high as female since males have up to twice as many flowers and bear the burden of galls (see VIII(A)). When the crown becomes decadent, due to age or an unfavourable habitat, epicormic shoots spring from the lower part of the trunk (Wardle [1961](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0630)).

### 6.1 Morphology, canopy leaves

> The canopy leaves of trees and older saplings are morphologically distinct from the juvenile leaves of seedlings, young saplings and epicormic and coppice shoots (see Wardle [1961](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0630) for details). Only sun leaves are developed in adult foliage due to the low canopy density; the leaf area index of ash is generally _c_. 3.5 compared to 4.6–5.2 in other competing species (Hölscher [2004](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0280)). Shaded saplings, however, have both sun and shade leaves; the latter are thinner and possess only one distinct palisade layer. Under heavy shade, the palisade mesophyll may be absent (Wardle [1961](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0630)). Young stems and leaves of ash are often tinged orange or purple (Rackham [2014](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0508)). Specific leaf area (SLA) has been measured at 13.8 ± 3.7 m2 kg−1 (assumed to be SD) in ash, compared to 22.4 ± 4.5 m2 kg−1 in _Ulmus glabra_ (Alberti _et al_. [2005](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0005)), fairly low in ash due to the presumed inclusion of the petiole and rachis. Hölscher ([2004](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0280)) found the SLA of ash in central Germany to vary with height: in the lower canopy (11 m above ground), leaves were thinner – 34.2 ± 2.0 m2 kg−1 (SD) – compared to the upper canopy (28 m tall trees) at 11.9 ± 0.8 m2 kg−1. Janse-ten Klooster, Thomas & Sterck ([2007](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0301)) found that saplings in deep shade with little direct sunlight had a leaf mass of 1.9 g compared to those in light shade (>10–100% full sunlight) at 3.4 g, and this was reflected in the SLA: 36.0 in shade and 25.3 m−2 kg−1 in light. Leaves formed 66% of biomass of a current-year shoot in ash compared to 74% in _Acer platanoides_ since there is more investment in stem and petioles (20 and 14%, respectively, of total shoot mass) in ash than in the maple (14 and 12%), so total leaf construction costs were lower in ash than _A. platanoides_; ash uses the leaves as cheap, disposable branches (Barthod & Epron [2005](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0038)). Leaf construction cost per unit area was higher in _A. platanoides_ than in ash (1.30 vs. 1.20 g glucose g−1) but decreased less with shade in ash, so shade leaves were proportionately more expensive in ash and may help explain their absence in mature trees. The petiole/rachis has a low lignin, structural polysaccharide and protein content and it has been calculated that the cost of its construction was 5% lower per unit mass than that for leaflets (Niinemets & Kull [1999](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0441); Niinemets [1999](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0440)). Niinemets ([1999](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0440)) also showed that the petiole had _c_. 10% the chlorophyll concentration of the leaflets, but the non-structural carbohydrate concentration was 20% higher than leaflets, suggesting that the petioles are used to store food. Stomata are found only on the underside of leaves (Morren [1866](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0429); Cornelissen _et al_. [2003](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0126)). Stomatal density varied with height: sapling 95 ± 32 mm−2 (SD), lower canopy 109 ± 20 mm−2, upper canopy 185 ± 36 mm−2 (Hölscher [2004](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0280)).

### 6.1 Morphology, trunk base and bark thickness

> On scree and unstable slopes, trees may become prostrate, at least at the base. This portion of the trunk produces adventitious roots on the underside so the lowest 1 m or so of the trunk may appear to be part of the root system rather than the trunk. In such cases, which can be hard to distinguish in older trees, it may be necessary to add up to 25 years (possibly more) to age estimates from ring counts at the apparent base of the tree (Merton [1970](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0412)). The bark of ash is thin. Köcher, Horna & Leuschner ([2012](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1365-2745.12566#jec12566-bib-0345)) measured bark thickness in trees 24–41 cm dbh as 7.56 ± 2.26 mm (SD), similar to that of _Fagus sylvatica_ and _Acer pseudoplatanus_.
