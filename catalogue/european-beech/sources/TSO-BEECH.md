## Cited passages

### Fagus sylvatica, description

> Tree to 50 m × 1(–1.3?) m dbh, parkland or coppiced trees considerably thicker. Bark pale grey. Young shoots silky haired at first, becoming more or less glabrous. Leaves oval-elliptic, apex acute, base rounded, (3–)4–10 cm × 2.5–4 cm, 5–8 pairs of veins, margins ciliate or remotely and very finely toothed (distinctly lobed in some cultivars), bright green above, pale green below, with a silky pubescence on both sides along midvein and primary veins at least when young; petiole 0.3–1 cm. Perianth of male flowers divided almost to base. Peduncles densely pubescent, 2.5 cm. Cupule _c._ 2.5 cm, scales are of one kind, needle-like ( [Tutin _et al_. (eds) 1993](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/flora-europaea-volume-1-psilotaceae-to-platanaceae-2nd-edition/); [Cullen _et al_. (eds) 2011](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/the-european-garden-flora-vol-iv-aquifoliaceae-to-hydrophyllaceae/)).

### Habitat

> **Habitat** Native in woods on well drained soils, especially in hills and mountains. Not naturally at home on poorly drained soils or in low-lying plains, etc., but extensively planted everywhere.

### Native range

> The species has a large native range, centred on central and western Europe where it can form extensive forests at lower elevations, blending into mixed coniferous forests and eventually giving way to conifers entirely at higher elevations. It is native to Scandinavia, at least in southern Sweden and south east Norway, and to Britain, having made it across just before the land bridge with continental Europe slipped beneath the waves. It extends east, to the north of the Black Sea as far as the mountains of the Crimea, and to the south-west it reaches the Pyrenees and the Cantabrian mountains of northern Spain ( [Krüssmann 1984](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/manual-of-cultivated-broad-leaved-trees-and-shrubs-1-a-d/); [Tutin _et al_. (eds) 1993](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/flora-europaea-volume-1-psilotaceae-to-platanaceae-2nd-edition/)). To the south-east, European Beech is native as far the Balkan peninsula, but here it blends into _F. orientalis,_ which extends from the eastern Balkans through Turkey, into the Caucasus, and hence to the Hyrcanian forests south of the Caspian in Iran. Hybrid forms occur where the two species meet ( [Davis 1984](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/the-flora-of-turkey-and-the-east-aegean-islands-vol-7/); [Kandemir & Kaya 2009](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/euforgen-technical-guidelines-for-genetic-conservation-and-use-of-oriental-beech-fagus-orientalis/)).

### Longevity in Britain and Ireland

> Alan Mitchell, that great measurer of trees, cautioned against the generation of extensive and definitive lists of the tallest and widest examples in the United Kingdom and Ireland given that Beech isn’t an especially long-lived tree there – 250 to 300 years is good going – and when older than 200 years trees are liable to die suddenly without much warning: ‘Unlike the oak, which takes an inordinate time to die, the beech does it over a weekend’ ( [Mitchell 1996](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/alan-mitchells-trees-of-britain/)). This being the case, ‘champion’ status is likely to move from tree to tree regularly, particularly as a large generation of trees planted in the United Kingdom and Ireland 200–250 years ago reaches senescence. Nevertheless, it is worth commenting on some remarkable trees that have been recorded in the past, and a small selection of the very best known today.

### Woodland-grown and open-grown trees

> All these are examples of trees growing in woodland, or near-woodland environments, hence the trees have been drawn up and have attained great height. Various commentators over the years have distinguished between this sort of Beech and ones that have been grown in open situations, for example in parkland or on lawns. One of the most remarkable ever recorded in the latter category is a tree that grew at Newbattle, Midlothian, Scotland. Elwes ( [1906](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/the-trees-of-great-britain-amp-ireland-vol-i-1906-vol-ii-1907-vol-iii-1908-vol-iv-1909-vol-v-1910-vol-vi-1912/)) described it as ‘the most remarkable, if not the largest, of all the Beeches of the spreading type now standing in Britain’. This enthusiastic commentary was on account of the lower branches of the tree, having layered when they reached the lawn on which the tree grew. Over one hundred years later the original trunk has long since died and decayed, but the layered branches have taken root and a semi-circle of new Beech trees provide a tantalising link to the past ( [Rodger, Stokes & Ogilvie 2006](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/ref/heritage-trees-of-scotland/)).
